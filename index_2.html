Use case 03

Survey-to-Action: a roadmap built from user feedback

Lucidya, an AI-native CX intelligence platform

Role
Senior UX Writer, research and roadmap owner
Years
2026
Scope
All products, Arabic & English
2surveys, 2 languages
4roadmap tasks

The challenge

We knew the copy had problems, but not which ones hurt users most. Instead of guessing, I asked the people who use and support the platform every day.

What I did

  1. Listen. Ran a bilingual internal survey across departments (31 responses) and an in-app survey for end users after about 5 minutes of use (18 responses).
  2. Prioritize. Turned the findings into an H2 2026 UX writing roadmap, ranking each issue by how often it was raised.
  3. Act. Shipped the roadmap one task at a time: error messages rewritten across all products, feature discoverability (empty states, tooltips, and navigation labels), an Arabic copy audit of key journeys where Arabic converted lower than English, and a terminology glossary with plain-language definitions.
  4. Measure. Relaunching both surveys at the end of 2026 to compare results with the first round.

What changed

Every writing task on the roadmap traces back to a real problem users reported, and the second survey round will show whether the fixes worked.

Use case 04

From “Something went wrong” to errors that help

Lucidya, an AI-native CX intelligence platform

Role
Senior UX Writer
Years
2026
Scope
Every error message, all products, Arabic & English
~140messages rewritten, AR & EN
8product areas

The challenge

In our UX writing surveys, unclear error messages were the top pain point. Too many failures ended in the same “Something went wrong,” so users couldn't tell what happened or what to do next, and they contacted support.

What I did

  1. Collect. Pulled every error message from the system's source files, in English and Arabic.
  2. Review. Traced the scenario behind each one: what triggers it, what the user was trying to do, and what they can do next.
  3. Rewrite. Rewrote each message to one pattern: what happened, why, and what to do next.
  4. Fill the gaps. Used support tickets to find cases the back end and front end never handled, which fell back to “Something went wrong,” and wrote specific messages for them.
  5. Ship. Launched with engineering in two phases: OmniServe in May (58 messages), then 7 more product areas in June (82 messages).

What changed

Error messages across the platform now follow one structure in both languages, and support tickets decreased after the launch. The pattern lives in the design system, and engineers now share new error cases during implementation so every one gets a proper message.

Use case 05

Naming things the way users do, in both languages

Lucidya, an AI-native CX intelligence platform

Role
Senior UX Writer and researcher
Years
2026
Scope
Product terminology and the Arabic interface
7user interviews
22EN/AR issues fixed in one flow

The challenge

Some product terms came from how the system is built, not how people think, so users didn't recognize them. In our surveys, confusing terminology and literal Arabic translation were recurring complaints, and Arabic users dropped off more than English users in key flows.

What I did

  1. Listen. Ran 7 interviews with agents and team leaders who work in the inbox every day, and used earlier research on how users connect their accounts.
  2. Rename from research. Replaced internal terms with the words users already say, instead of choosing names internally.
  3. Audit the Arabic. Reviewed the Monitor creation flow, where Arabic users dropped off most, and found 22 English and Arabic issues, from literal translations to inconsistent terms.
  4. Write Arabic natively. Fixed each issue in natural Modern Standard Arabic instead of translating from English, and documented terms in the EN/AR product glossary.

What changed

Product terms now match the words users use, and all 22 fixes in the Arabic Monitor creation flow are live. Naming decisions are now based on research, and the squads apply this approach in their day-to-day work.
